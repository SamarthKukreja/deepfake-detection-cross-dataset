Why do deepfake detectors fail in the real world?

We trained five detection models on one dataset and tested them on completely different ones. Here's what we found — and why it changes how you should think about building a reliable deepfake detector.

The problem

High accuracy in the lab doesn't mean it works in the wild.

Most deepfake detection papers report impressive numbers — but only on the same dataset they trained on. The moment you deploy that detector against a new manipulation method or a different video source, performance can drop to coin-flip level.

The core question this study answers

When preprocessing, training recipe, and evaluation are held fixed, how much does the choice of backbone actually matter for cross-dataset generalization? And does training-set diversity matter more than architecture?

Why identity-disjoint splits matter

Many published results accidentally leak identity information — the same person appears in both training and test. We used the official FaceForensics++ source-video partition so no source video's crops appear in both train and test, making the numbers honest.

What "video-level" evaluation means

We average all face-crop scores from a video into one score before computing AUC. This is more realistic — a real detector gives one verdict per video, not per crop.

Best backbone AUC per experiment. Reverse transfer collapses to near-chance for all five models.

What we built

One pipeline. Five backbones. Four experiments.

The key design choice: everything stays the same except the backbone. Same faces, same splits, same optimizer, same augmentation. That way, any difference in results is from the architecture — not from hidden hyper-parameter tuning.

How the pipeline works

1

Sample frames

Extract every 10th frame from each video across FF++, Celeb-DF, and DFD.

2

Detect faces

MTCNN finds and crops faces (confidence ≥ 0.95, box ≥ 60 px, 20% margin expanded, 224×224 resize).

3

Assign splits

All crops from one source video stay in the same split — no identity leakage between train and test.

4

Fine-tune backbone

AdamW + cosine annealing + label smoothing (ε=0.05) + progressive unfreezing. Up to 30 epochs, patience 5.

5

Video-level score

5-view TTA (flip, rotation, brightness), average crop scores per video, then compute AUC with bootstrap CIs.

Key findings

What the results actually tell us.

These are the four takeaways that change how you should think about building a deployable deepfake detector.

⚡

Training diversity matters more than architecture

Exp 3 (Celeb-DF → FF++) collapses to near-chance for every single backbone — including the best-performing ones. Training on a larger, more diverse dataset is what drives cross-dataset performance, not picking a fancier model.

🏆

ConvNeXt-Tiny leads — but not by much

It has the highest AUC point estimates in Exp 1, 2, and 4. But under paired statistical tests, the advantage over ResNet-50 is not significant at this single training seed. Don't treat architecture rankings as absolute.

🔬

Transformers underperform CNNs here

ViT-Base scores lower than ConvNeXt-Tiny and ResNet-50 in every experiment. Under limited training data, the convolutional inductive bias beats the attention mechanism for this task.

🌊

Noise is the biggest corruption threat

Under the robustness study (JPEG, blur, noise, downscaling), additive noise causes the worst AUC drops — to as low as 0.49 on the DFD detector. The models were trained on clean images and don't generalise to degraded ones.

The bigger picture

What this means for anyone building a real detector.

If you're building a deepfake detector for moderation, forensic triage, or identity verification, the results here have a clear implication: train on as many different datasets and manipulation methods as you can get. A model that tops FaceForensics++ benchmarks is not automatically production-ready. ConvNeXt-Tiny is a solid backbone choice — but the data you train on will matter more than the architecture you pick.
